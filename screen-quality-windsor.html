What's Actually Going Into Your Phone

Two repairs can cost £40 apart and look identical on the counter. The difference is the panel, and almost nobody explains it. Here is the whole thing — substrate, brightness, thickness, True Tone — including where the cheap option is genuinely the right call.

The One-Paragraph Version

Every iPhone from the X onwards left the factory with an OLED panel built on a flexible plastic substrate. When you replace that screen you have three realistic choices: a soft OLED, which is built the same way; a hard OLED, which is the same display technology on a rigid glass base; or an incell LCD, which is a different display technology altogether, wearing your phone's frame.

We fit soft OLED as standard, from MobileSentrix. Most cheap repairs fit incell. Both carry a warranty and both work. They are not the same thing, and you should be told which one you're getting before it goes in — not after.

Why the Substrate Is the Whole Story

This is the bit that gets skipped. Your iPhone's original OLED is built on polyimide — a flexible plastic film. That matters for three practical reasons:

  • Thickness. A flexible substrate lets the display electronics pack tighter, so the finished assembly is thinner and sits properly in the housing. A rigid glass panel has to be thicker to survive, and can stand slightly proud of the frame.
  • Flex tolerance. A phone chassis flexes — in your pocket, when you sit down, when it's being fitted. Polyimide tolerates that. Rigid glass is more vulnerable to cracking under uneven pressure, including pressure applied during a clumsy installation.
  • It's what the phone was designed around. Apple engineered the frame, adhesive channel and cable routing for a flexible panel. Fitting one matched to that design is simply less of a compromise.

Soft OLED is not a marketing tier. It is a description of how the panel is physically built — the same construction family as the one that came out of the factory.

Where we'll argue against ourselves: construction type alone does not determine brightness, colour accuracy or pixel uniformity. A well-made hard OLED can measurably outperform a poorly made soft OLED. "Soft OLED" on an invoice is not automatically a guarantee — the manufacturer and the batch matter just as much as the category. Anyone telling you the label alone settles it is selling, not explaining.

What Incell Actually Costs You

We're not going to pretend incell is worthless. It works, we fit it on request, and it carries the same 12-month warranty. On a five-year-old handset that has stopped getting security updates, spending soft OLED money is a poor decision and we'll say so.

But you should know what changes, because it is not subtle once you've seen it:

Blacks become grey

OLED pixels switch off individually. An LCD backlight is always on behind the panel, so black is dark grey. Most obvious at night and in dark mode.

Dimmer outdoors

Lower peak brightness. In direct Windsor sunshine on the Long Walk, the difference is immediate.

Colour shift

Whites often read cooler or warmer than the original, which you notice most when comparing against another phone.

True Tone can be lost

Support varies by batch on incell. On soft and hard OLED it can be transferred from the old panel.

Battery behaviour changes

OLED draws less on dark content because black pixels are off. An LCD backlight doesn't care what's on screen.

Resale value

Anyone buying a used handset checks the screen first. A visibly non-original display is a negotiating point against you.

How to Check What You Were Given

Already had a repair somewhere and wondering? Four tests, no tools:

  • Black test. Open a full-screen black image in a dark room. OLED goes properly black. Incell glows dark grey, and you'll see the backlight bleed at the edges.
  • Sunlight test. Take it outside on a bright day and turn brightness to maximum. A soft OLED stays readable. An incell struggles.
  • True Tone. Settings → Display & Brightness, or long-press the brightness slider in Control Centre. If the toggle has vanished entirely, the calibration data was not transferred.
  • Flush test. Run a fingernail from the frame onto the glass. A thicker rigid assembly can catch slightly.

None of this means you were cheated — a budget panel honestly sold at a budget price is a fair deal. It means you should know what's in there, and what to ask for next time.

Screen Quality FAQs

What is a soft OLED screen?

An aftermarket panel built on flexible polyimide — the same construction family as the factory Apple panel. Thinner, sits flush, tolerates chassis flex. Industry grading puts it at roughly 90–95% of original display quality. It's what we fit as standard on OLED iPhones, from MobileSentrix.

Soft OLED vs hard OLED — what's the difference?

The substrate. Soft is flexible polyimide like the original; hard is rigid glass, slightly thicker and less flex-tolerant. Hard OLED is cheaper and its rigid build resists some pressure well. Category alone doesn't decide brightness or colour — a good hard OLED beats a bad soft OLED — but at equal quality, soft is the closer match to factory.

Why is incell so much cheaper?

It's LCD technology in an OLED phone's body. Backlight instead of self-lit pixels, so no true blacks, lower peak brightness, washes out in sun. Often a third or less of soft OLED at wholesale — which is exactly why cheap repairs default to it.

Will True Tone still work?

It depends on calibration data stored on the original panel, which can be transferred across with a programmer. Soft and hard OLED support this. Whether it works on your model depends on the handset and the condition of the old display — we check and tell you before, not after.

What screens do you actually buy?

They ship with the steel plate pre-installed and fresh water-seal adhesive, and XO7 2.0 carries a lifetime supplier warranty covering flex, digitizer, frame separation and panel failure.
